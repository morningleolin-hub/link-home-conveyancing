Transfer of Property Ownership

A transfer of property ownership may be needed when ownership shares change, a property is transferred between family members, spouses or related entities, or a title needs to be updated following a particular life or business event. Although some transfers are described as gifts or made without payment, the legal, duty, tax and lending consequences depend on the circumstances and the way the transaction is structured. We assist with the conveyancing documents and registration process and help identify issues that should be checked before the transfer proceeds.

Understanding the proposed transfer

The first step is to understand who currently owns the property, who is intended to become the registered proprietor, what share is changing and why the transfer is proposed. We may need to review a current title, the parties’ identification details, any mortgage or caveat, and documents explaining the arrangement. Transfers can arise from changes between co-owners, family arrangements, relationship breakdown, estate-related events, trust or company structures, or a change in the proportions held by proprietors. Different circumstances can require different supporting documents or procedures. It is important to explain the actual arrangement rather than relying only on a label such as “gift”, “nomination” or “no consideration”.

Duty, tax and finance considerations

A transfer without cash consideration is not automatically exempt from Victorian land transfer duty. The State Revenue Office may require a duty assessment and supporting information, and any exemption or concession depends on the applicable legislation and the facts. Capital gains tax, land tax, foreign purchaser issues, trust or company consequences and other tax questions may also arise, but these are not determined solely by the conveyancing documents.Where tax or financial implications arise, we can identify that these may require advice from an appropriately qualified specialist. If a mortgage is registered, the lender’s consent, refinance, discharge or replacement loan arrangements may be required. The lender must be involved early because changing the registered ownership without dealing with the mortgage arrangements may not be possible or appropriate.

Documents and registration

Depending on the transaction, the process may involve a transfer of land, verification of identity, client authorisation, digital duties information, supporting declarations, lender documents and electronic lodgement. The parties’ names and capacity must be correct—for example, an individual, company or trustee capacity should not be assumed interchangeable. We coordinate the conveyancing information and work with the other parties and relevant service providers to prepare the transaction for lodgement.Where tax or financial implications arise, we can identify that these may require advice from an appropriately qualified specialist.

Timing and practical issues

A transfer can take longer where the parties’ instructions are incomplete, the title contains a mortgage or caveat, the lender needs to approve a change, duty information is outstanding, or additional documents are required. Before work begins, we can outline the expected steps, information needed and likely disbursements. We will not assume that a transfer is duty-free or that no tax consequences arise without an appropriate basis. If the proposed transfer is part of a broader agreement—such as a property settlement or business restructure—tell us so that the conveyancing process can be coordinated with the relevant professional advisers.

What to provide

Useful starting documents include a current title or property address, the full legal names of all current and proposed owners, the proposed ownership shares, details of consideration or any gift arrangement, mortgage information and the reason for the transfer. Provide any relevant court order, binding financial agreement, trust deed, company documents or estate documents if applicable. The exact requirements depend on the facts.Where tax or financial implications arise, we can identify that these may require advice from an appropriately qualified specialist.
